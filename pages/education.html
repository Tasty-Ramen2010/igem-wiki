Elementary School

Midway Elementary School STEM Night

As a part of our community educational outreach, our team participated in a STEM night, hosted at Midway Elementary School, where high school students volunteered to guide elementary students through interactive science experiments and activities. As a part of our efforts, members of Denmark's iGEM team volunteered at several activity stations, assisting students as they explored various scientific concepts through hands-on experiments. By participating in this event, our team was able to engage with younger students and encourage curiosity in STEM from an early age. Throughout the event, our team members worked directly with students by explaining the activities, answering questions, and helping them understand the scientific concepts behind each experiment. Rather than simply providing instructions, we encouraged students to make observations, share ideas, and discuss what they thought would happen before completing their activity.

By listening to the students’ questions and observing how they approached each activity, we gained insight into how scientific concepts can be communicated to younger audiences. For example, when a student had difficulty understanding a topic (ex. The Water Cycle), we found that relating the concept to something they could observe helped them understand it better. As this was our first outreach event specifically involving younger students, the experience encouraged our team to set out to create more educational events like our children’s book, The Money Tree Mystery, and our board game, BioRails. Through this experience, our team was able to promote STEM education while improving our ability to make science accessible and engaging for elementary students.

BioRails

We developed BioRails, an education board game designed to introduce younger students to malaria, prevention, and some of the science behind our iGEM project. Through interactive gameplay, players navigate a track, collect train parts, and answer malaria-related biology trivia questions, and encounter challenges inspired by malaria prevention. The purpose of our game was to make malaria education more accessible and engaging for younger audiences while introducing them to scientific concepts in a fun and collaborative environment.

We recognized the importance of making scientific education accessible to students beyond the traditional classroom setting, so we started with malaria. While our project focuses on improving malaria diagnostics through synthetic biology, we wanted to extend our work beyond and make malaria education accessible to younger audiences. We saw an opportunity to introduce students to the impact of malaria, how it spreads, and the importance of prevention and diagnosis in a way that's both engaging and appropriate for their age level. By creating an education board game, we aimed to make learning about malaria more interactive and encourage students to become more aware of global health crises.

During the development of BioRails, we designed a board game in which players must collect four train components and reach the end of the track to win. Throughout the game, players encounter challenges such as chance cards and biology trivia questions that incorporate concepts related to malaria and its prevention. For example, mosquito sets and insecticide are added to the gameplay to introduce prevention strategies, while trivia questions help enhance the players knowledge on the topic. By integrating these concepts into familiar board game mechanics, we aimed to make learning about malaria fun. Through BioRails, we hope to expand awareness of malaria and introduce students above the age of 3 to the importance of disease prevention, early diagnosis, and scientific innovation.

Board Game Night

An educational and informational event, called “Board Game Night,” accompanied the creation and establishment of our board game series, BioRails. Taking place on August 22nd, 2026, the purpose of this event was to introduce the board game to a younger audience as a unique way of learning about synthetic biology concepts such as antibiotic resistance, origin of replication, and coding sequences. This event was hosted at the Denmark Public Library, and each of the ~15 attendees were given the opportunity to play the board game along with other popular board games such as UNO and “Sorry!”

Before the event itself, our team encouraged students who were in both secondary and post-secondary school to serve as volunteers with the aim of assisting young children with assembling BioRails as well as other similar board games. During the event, specifically during the introduction, members from Human Practices presented an overview of the project, including the development of two new software programs, LATTE and Hybridock-Pep. Additionally, the development of our new board game was explained in terms of advancing our project and creating an entertaining way for a wide range of individuals to understand the basis of our project’s education mission while having fun.

Piney Grove Workshops

As one of our early educational initiatives, our team collaborated with Piney Grove Middle School in order to promote synthetic biology education through workshops designed for 7-8th grade students. These synthetic biology seminars took place 4/22/2026, 4/23/2026, and 4/24/2026. Our goal was to spread the awareness of synthetic biology needed to cultivate early interest in related careers. As such, our workshops corresponded with the following structure: an initial, 5-10 minute presentation regarding synthetic biology’s relevance, related careers, prevalence in daily lives, global impact, and significance in the iGEM competition, followed by a roughly 20 minute lab-based activity. The initial presentation served as an interactive introduction to synthetic biology with casual questions and statements, such as “Have you noticed the GMO labels prevalent on the foods you buy?” or “Raise your hand if you have seen an abnormally large fruit” in order to cultivate interest in the new topic for students.

Figure 1.1. Teammates Presenting for Attendees

Afterwards, we continued with a mini lab-based activity to introduce the workshop attendees to real lab skills. This activity was “micropipette art” in which participants were taught how to use a micropipette and then used this skill to pick up and intentionally place various droplets of colored water. Not only does this serve as an activity to introduce interest in synthetic biology, but this activity also introduces the precision and technical skills necessary to contribute in synthetic biology lab settings.

Figure 1.2. Attendees Participating in the Mini-lab Micropipette Lab Activity

Our goal through this workshop was to offer a unique lab experience to attendees in order to encourage scientific interest and to provide attendees with a baseline skill to expand on, in other synthetic biology experiences. In order to determine whether or not we achieved this solution, our team spoke to Mrs. Smith, the teacher we coordinated with to develop these workshops, and asked whether or not these workshops held any educational value and variance from regular science-based coursework. Her response detailed how our workshops varied from the coursework she taught and instilled industry-valuable skills in attendees, therefore achieving our initial goals.

Figure 1.3. Attendee’s Micropipette Art

However, we recognized the high interest and learning potential within the field of synthetic biology, and decided to expand on this on a larger scale. As such, we hosted our Biotech Bootcamp, a week-long camp for middle schoolers to learn more lab-skills and translatable experiments, labs, and scientific activities. We expanded on our pre-existing activity from the Piney Grove workshops to create more activities to expand on synthetic biology educational value for middle schoolers.

Synthetic Biology Course

Using the feedback that was obtained from the production and application of our synthetic biology standards and lesson plans (specifically the “Plasmid Bracelet” lesson plan), our team constructed an open-source course that utilized factors such as informational videos, quizzes, presentations, and learning objectives to accurately use science communication to educate individuals about various, common synthetic biology concepts.

In order to construct a course that was free to use and available to the general public, research was conducted to identify course websites that our team could use for a feasible amount. However, this process was difficult and our team decided to pivot to constructing the components of the course instead, such as creating presentation slideshows, video recordings, and quizzes.

The open-source accessibility of our team’s synthetic biology course allowed for individuals across the internet to easily access and learn about synthetic biology concepts that are crucial to understand and master in order to perform wet lab via iGEM. Because the course was published online, it provided easy access to individuals who were not able to attend some of our team’s hosted workshops, camps, and webinars in-person. This also allowed any interested applicants for our next year's team to gain more experience and understanding around these topics.
