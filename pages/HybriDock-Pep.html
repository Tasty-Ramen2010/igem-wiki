01Overview

Background Information

Nearly all binder-based diagnostic tests rely on small molecules, binders that are designed to fit in grooves and pockets across proteins and create strong bonds between them. However, the small molecule-based binder tests can only detect a minority of all proteins, with the human body’s proteome only having 15% of its proteins be able to bind with small molecules. This percentage of proteins with plausible small molecules only grows smaller, as many diseases have begun to evolve out of them, an example being malaria’s HRP-II protein, already not found in more than 45 million infected people.

However, there does seem to be a solution: computationally designed de novo peptides–short chains of amino acids, generally under 25 amino acids long that are generated through computational methods and cannot be found in nature. These peptides are highly coveted as binders due to their variety coming from their enormous amount of possible combinations and their ability to bind to virtually any protein. Furthermore, with the vast amount of configurations, variable geometry and flexibility and sheer amount of peptide combinations, they are the perfect choice to create protein-specific, high-affinity binders, unique to each protein.

Personal Experience & Academic Feedback

The development, optimization, and computational modelling of peptide binders have always been gated by large corporations and prestigious universities due to their requirement of large computing clusters, along with slow, iterative computing algorithms, and an accuracy <10% for developed peptides. Furthermore, old Monte Carlo algorithms iterate through billions of different possible peptide poses, still unable to cover all poses.

During our work on peptides during the start, we relied on softwares like AlphaFold, ESMFold, Boltz-2, and we identified a key issue: the current software was incredibly unoptimized for peptides. Once we had our own doubts, we referred to academia to identify more problems regarding the computational development of peptides and it gave us shocking revelations:

“We had a team of like 12 people working on exactly the project (peptide development) you’re working on... after getting 12 people on it, our conclusion is it’s really hard to find data and most of it’s mislabeled” - Dr. Brian Coventry

“...most of the softwares … aren't really super accurate to protein and peptide interactions. …under 30 amino acids, the accuracy of alpha fold dropped significantly ... ” - our team, in meetings with Dr. Hosseinzadeh and with Drs. Maghsoud and Quintero

Through academic feedback and our own experience, we found the primary issue: there doesn’t exist a platform to effectively and accurately design peptide binders that include specialized docking and scoring.

Solution

In order to combat these issues, we made HybriDock-Pep over the course of 6 months. HybriDock-Pep is a novel ML-based docking and affinity scoring tool. We used a modified and improved upon version of RAPiDock as our base, using its features: bi-conformational graph encoding and SE(3)-equivariant denoising, along with our own custom GPU kernels and support for speed on x86 and ARM-based machines. Then, we paired it with our physics-based ML model to score protein-peptide interactions in kcal/mol with an average error of about 1.4 kcal/mol on leakage-free tests, at a fraction of the compute cost of free-energy simulations and an even smaller amount of time needed.

02One Program Workflow

HybriDock-Pep is relatively easy to understand: it is divided into 4 steps, covering pose generation, ranking, clustering and finally scoring. It takes input in the form of a protein’s PDB structure and peptide sequences, and runs the entire pipeline and outputs the relevant development data, spanning ΔG, optimal binding spots, strongest interactions and more.

Fig. 1 | The stages of HybriDock-Pep.

Stage 1: diffusion sampling

The initial stage of our model is pose generation, the process in which our software folds and docks the folded peptide onto the protein in its calculated most optimal spot. In order to do this, we borrow RAPiDock, an all-atom SE(3) equivariant diffusion model with specialized Clebsch-Gordan architecture to maintain homogeneity across the 3-dimensional plane along with an optimized bi-conformational graph encoder for the peptide.

Fig. 2 | The sampler's network. Three graphs and a noise embedding enter four equivariant layers, and four heads output the scores that drive each diffusion step.

Using advanced mathematical models, it places the peptide in a random place with a random structure and “denoises” or nudges the peptide to its most favourable position and structure.

Fig. 3 | One run of the sampler, step by step. Five of the sixteen steps are shown, and the faint copies are other runs at the same step. The drawings are an illustration, but the noise values and the schedule underneath are the real ones.

Furthermore, we reverse engineered the original code to make it work on different systems including Windows and MacOS, while also writing custom kernels to speed up generation on these devices. For example, our post-modification model generates poses every 0.76 seconds on ~$600 consumer laptops. However, we believe our most important contribution is fine tuning the original model so it performs significantly better on longer peptides, and on proteins which conformationally change upon binding (statistics regarding that can be found in the Results section).

Stages 1.5 to 3: relief, ranking and clustering

While diffusion models are incredible at putting peptides where they belong, they fail to place side chains in exactly the right places without causing clashes, and stage 1 of HybriDock-Pep is no exception to this. Therefore, steps are taken in order to convert poses into their most stable and physically accurate forms. First, each and every pose runs a light clash-relief run through OpenMM with AMBER ff14SB force field and implicit solvent. Heavy atoms are held with strong springs, roughly 50,000 kJ/mol/nm² or 60 kcal/mol per ångström of movement, and that ensures poses only move when required to. Also, we remove poses that move more than 0.5 Å, as it is an indication of an irregular pose.

Poses are then converted to the PDBQT format with Meeko and handed off to AutoDock Vina. Vina is used for its local optimizer in order to separate poses that clash and don’t clash with the protein, removed when any pair of heavy atoms is closer than 1.5 Å. Then, BSA-fit and a ML-based ranker both rank the output data, one sorts by the amount of interactions while penalizing clashes, while the other uses machine learning. We used these over Rosetta’s ranker as it is far faster for the same ranking power. Finally, each pose is reduced to individual Cα atoms near the protein, a pairwise RMSD matrix is built for each, and average-linkage clustering groups the poses. The silhouette score is used to find clusters and find the best of each. An optional step also allows to relax each pose with MM-GBSA but stays turned off on default due to its computational cost.

Fig. 4 | What each stage hands to the next. Sampling runs in its own software environment (dashed box). The labels on the arrows are the files that pass between stages.

Stage 3.6: the binding energy

Following minimization, a specialized machine-learning physics-informed affinity model, a histogram-based gradient-boosted decision-tree ensemble, runs to predict the ΔG kcal/mol of the static pose. The model is a specially calibrated model that uses a 262 dimensional matrix, containing 16 geometric features like Shrake-Rupley surface area in bound and unbound states, hydrogen and salt bound area, Miyazawa-Jerningan contact potential, heavy atom-weight burial hotspot terms, 220 descriptors peptide descriptors from electrostatics to hydrophobicity to polarity, and 25 terms consisting of specialized desolvation and water dynamic-compensating features, and a gradient weighted interaction pooled term known as interaction fingerprinting.

Together, they produce affinity scores between the protein and peptide with an MAE of ~1.39 kcal/mol on 30%-id clustered leakage-free dataset on 895 protein-peptide complexes from a well known dataset known as PDBind (for crystal scoring). We also ship an AI-based scoring model that scores for poses we generate instead of crystal ones, optimized for poses that we generate. It leans less on contact features and allows for the strength of bonds over slightly greater design in order to model the ΔG in kcal/mol in a relaxed peptide conformation of the same computationally designed peptide structure.

We use our own scorer as we identified that other softwares perform poorly for peptides in predicting ΔG in kcal/mol, which we go more in depth in our results section.

Fig. 5 | From a pose to a ΔG. Features are routed by peptide length to one of two models.

Blind docking

So far, the general pipeline of the software is built on the assumption that the user knows the optimal binding coordinates of the peptide binding spot. However, we do have an option to address this, activated by leaving out the “--site” and adding “--blind” to the command or as a specialized option in the web UI. In order to make this succeed, we run a specialized global peptide docking model with 3 times as much as the poses the user specifies, each from a vastly greater range of total seeds, therefore we get the highest variety. Then, based on Cα centroids of those poses, they are grouped into one of 3 candidate sites ranked by the amount of poses there. In order to further boost accuracy for this method, the software re-runs using those 3 spots as local binding spot coordinates, using 150-poses for each of them, making sure to prioritize accuracy in this run. Blind docking generally takes 5 times longer than regular but offers vastly more accuracy. In fact, compared to general blind docking which places peptides <5Å around ~66% of the time, the new general architecture places peptides <5Å around 85% of the time on a test set of 120 proteins.

Selectivity

Since our software is being applied in the general sense of the scientific community, we also offer exclusive functions that make it easier to modify and design binders for certain situations. One major example is selectivity, where the software takes 2 protein structures as the input and runs the pipeline on both of them before reporting the ΔΔG = ΔG(target) − ΔG(off-target) with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval over the top cluster representatives. ΔΔG is used to eliminate the systemic bias that comes with the scoring function as the unmodeled desolvation of the peptide alone is cancelled with this and a more accurate representation of how the peptide will bind is known.

Fig. 6 | From about 100 poses to a selectivity decision. The scores are made up for illustration. The steps, the ΔΔG definition and the bootstrap are how the software works.

Outputs

Each run stores the output data in a variety of files: ranked_poses.csv (data ranking each peptide and scoring breakdown), best_pose.pdb, cluster_summary.csv, convergence_plot.png and silhouette_plot.png (imaging data of how the clusters were picked) and run_metadata.json.

03Implementation Details

Install

On macOS, Linux or Windows (through WSL2), installation is three commands:

  • clone the repository, move into the folder, and run ./install.sh (or install.bat on Windows, which sets up WSL2).
  • The script installs everything, including conda, PyTorch and RAPiDock, in about 15 to 30 minutes.
  • Afterwards, activate the environment with conda activate score-env.
  • To update, run git pull, git submodule update --init --recursive and ./install.sh again.
  • To remove everything, run ./uninstall.sh, or ./uninstall.sh --all to delete the folder too.

We also include a Colab notebook for people who wish to try the software without using so much of it. The Colab notebook seamlessly integrates with the free tier T4 GPU given by Colab Notebook. Furthermore, this allows for people to use the software on nearly any device with an internet connection. It does take ~17 minutes to compile and build all the required files, while also downloading a ESM-2 language model, but docking is relatively shorter, around 5 minutes once the first time building has been done.

A first run

To check an installation, score a known complex. This takes a few seconds and needs no sampling:

hybridock-pep crystal-score \
    --receptor data/pdbs/1YCR_mdm2.pdb \
    --peptide-pdb data/pdbs/1YCR_peptide.pdb \
    --peptide ETFSDLWKLLPE

A general run can give a ΔG of anywhere from -8 to -10 kcal/mol with -8.5 being the experimental wetlab value. To run a real docking:

hybridock-pep dock \
    --peptide ETFSDLWKLLPE \
    --receptor data/pdbs/1YCR_mdm2.pdb \
    --site 25.20 -25.61 -7.97 \
    --box 30 \
    --n-samples 20 \
    --output-dir runs/demo

Website and terminal UI

Other than the command line interface, HybriDock-Pep can also be run through a terminal-based app and as a web application. The website can be run on any machine where the software is installed after running “./launch_web.sh”, opening the web app at http://127.0.0.1:8000. The web app offers a way to use the entire software, keeping all the primary features of the software and giving the user access to them in a simplified way, without heavy difficulty. The terminal UI can be used with “./launch_ui.sh”

Testing and reproducibility

The repository has 64 test files in order to check the functionality of the entire software to reduce tampering and also has a running track of 365 experiments that were used over the course of 3 months to develop parts like the scoring function and finetuned docking model. In order to reproduce old commands, you can run “reproducibility” along with it in the command line interface to run the same pipeline over multiple seeds and get a secondary metric to check average ΔG kcal/mol between then and the agreeing RMSD of the top poses. The code and data are archived on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.21764713 and 10.5281/zenodo.21680573).

04Results & Validation

How we benchmark

Throughout our search to identify the best protein-peptide scoring tools, we saw many tools that reported low MAE and high correlation like from PPI-Affinity, ADCP, HADDOCK, and more. But when we tested them on the latest data from the past few years, the models fail and congregate in their collective correlation around r=~0.3. So in order to make sure our scores reported do not look inflated, we cluster complexes by their sequence identity and remove those, along with homologous clusters. Then, using data from papers and from the official installation, we either downloaded different softwares, or made clones of them to our best extent, for example one of PPI-Affinity as it had been taken down.

For poses, we used a set of 247 protein-peptide complexes filtered against our training data, and a larger length-balanced set of 387 complexes to see how each method behaves from short to long peptides. All of the data is leakage-free.

Current Drawbacks

While we claim all these advancements due to our software, we do have to state some things in order to not overclaim. First of all, our scoring function is not perfect, and in fact struggles heavily with extremely long peptides, peptides that create conformational changes, and peptides that are charged too. The training data for protein-peptide complexes with their dissociation constants is relatively minute, sitting at around 3,000 total complexes and ~1,700 usable complexes. Even so, a portion of them come out to be related >90% accuracy and even when the same pair is repeated across different datasets, the ΔΔG between them can be as high as 0.82 kcal/mol, showing a ceiling in the data we provided. Our talks with Dr. Coventry only added evidence to this as he claims that finding data for this class is incredibly hard and even when 12 of his lab members tried in years past, they simply could not find this much information. Based on all this information, we reported out true and honest score down below.

MDM2 and p53, as a worked example

When originally developing the software, I wanted to design a general use case. I chose MDM2 and the p53 peptide (ETFSDLWKLLPE) as a standard case as it is a widely known structure and is confirmed with it’s ΔG being -8.5 kcal/mol verified experimentally from multiple scores. Our scoring function reflects this when running this common example when given the site, placing the peptide 0.80 Å from the crystal pose, against 3.54 Å for DiffPepDock, while generally scoring it to be anywhere from -8 to -10 kcal/mol.

The Coventry grid

In the middle of September, we reached out to Dr. Brian Coventry from the University of Washington, and he responded to us with a challenge: given 18 proteins and 18 peptides, reproduce a binding matrix and score each of them from Wu et al. (Science, 2025, Fig. 2B).

During the beginning of the challenge we struggled a lot after working for nearly 3 hours, but through the application of other computational tools in conjunction with ours, we were able to solve the issue.

Following our ordeal, we were able to consistently use our software to identify the most optimal protein-peptide pair in the top-5 for 15 of the 18 protein-peptide optimal pairs as shown in the image on the right where we show our 48 top pics for each peptide-protein complex. We failed on pc18, n4, and pc26 – proteins with deep, conformationally changing grooves.

Fig. 9 | The 18 × 18 specificity grid. Left: the measured ground truth from Wu et al. (2025), Fig. 2B. Right: our shortlist, with each row drawn only as deep as you would have to test to reach the true partner. One dark cell means we called the true partner first.

Throughout our tests, we identified the main issue to be that most of these were de novo designed proteins and peptide binders. While our software really struggled with this at first, we were able to use co-folding from Boltz-2 in conjunction with our diffusion generation model to optimize our model to make quick and accurate predictions. Furthermore, we were able to identify that our software struggled to make accurate predictions for de-novo proteins with deep, conformational grooves, and for co-folding dependent proteins.

Through this endeavor, we were also able to identify key strengths and weaknesses of our own project, allowing us to take the feedback by Dr. Coventry in order to re-train our AI model and optimize our scoring function, while adding specialized features to speed up the production and development of binders of our own.
