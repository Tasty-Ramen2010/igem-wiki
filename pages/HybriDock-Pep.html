01Overview

Background Information

Nearly all binder-based diagnostic tests rely on small molecules, binders that are designed to fit in grooves and pockets across proteins and create strong bonds between them. However, the small molecule-based binder tests can only detect a minority of all proteins, with the human body’s proteome only having 15% of its proteins be able to bind with small molecules. This percentage of proteins with plausible small molecules only grows smaller, as many diseases have begun to evolve out of them, an example being malaria’s HRP-II protein, already not found in more than 45 million infected people.

However, there does seem to be a solution: computationally designed de novo peptides–short chains of amino acids, generally under 25 amino acids long that are generated through computational methods and cannot be found in nature. These peptides are highly coveted as binders due to their variety coming from their enormous amount of possible combinations and their ability to bind to virtually any protein. Furthermore, with the vast amount of configurations, variable geometry and flexibility and sheer amount of peptide combinations, they are the perfect choice to create protein-specific, high-affinity binders, unique to each protein.

Personal Experience & Academic Feedback

The development, optimization, and computational modelling of peptide binders have always been gated by large corporations and prestigious universities due to their requirement of large computing clusters, along with slow, iterative computing algorithms, and an accuracy <10% for developed peptides. Furthermore, old Monte Carlo algorithms iterate through billions of different possible peptide poses, still unable to cover all poses.

During our work on peptides during the start, we relied on softwares like AlphaFold, ESMFold, Boltz-2, and we identified a key issue: the current software was incredibly unoptimized for peptides. Once we had our own doubts, we referred to academia to identify more problems regarding the computational development of peptides and it gave us shocking revelations:

“We had a team of like 12 people working on exactly the project (peptide development) you’re working on... after getting 12 people on it, our conclusion is it’s really hard to find data and most of it’s mislabeled” - Dr. Brian Coventry

“...most of the softwares … aren't really super accurate to protein and peptide interactions. …under 30 amino acids, the accuracy of alpha fold dropped significantly ... ” - our team, in meetings with Dr. Hosseinzadeh and with Drs. Maghsoud and Quintero

Through academic feedback and our own experience, we found the primary issue: there doesn’t exist a platform to effectively and accurately design peptide binders that include specialized docking and scoring.

Solution

In order to combat these issues, we made HybriDock-Pep over the course of 6 months. HybriDock-Pep is a novel ML-based docking and affinity scoring tool. We used a modified and improved upon version of RAPiDock as our base, using its features: bi-conformational graph encoding and SE(3)-equivariant denoising, along with our own custom GPU kernels and support for speed on x86 and ARM-based machines. Then, we paired it with our physics-based ML model to score protein-peptide interactions in kcal/mol with an average error of about 1.4 kcal/mol on leakage-free tests, at a fraction of the compute cost of free-energy simulations and an even smaller amount of time needed.

02One Program Workflow

HybriDock-Pep takes one peptide sequence and one protein structure and runs the whole path to a binding energy with a single command. The path has four phases, pose generation, ranking, clustering and scoring, and every stage writes its results to disk so you can look at what each stage handed to the next.

Fig. 1 | HybriDock-Pep from input to output. A peptide sequence and a protein structure with a binding site enter on the left. The sampler, a diffusion model with an equivariant network and four score heads, turns a random starting peptide into about 100 poses over sixteen iterations. Cleanup, ranking and clustering follow, and the affinity model predicts ΔG. The layout follows the style of the RAPiDock schematic (Zhao et al., 2025), which our sampler builds on; the drawing is our own.

Stage 1: diffusion sampling

The first stage is our retrained version of RAPiDock, a diffusion model that generates all-atom poses of a peptide on a protein. A diffusion model learns to undo noise. During training, real complexes are scrambled a little at a time, and the network learns which way each part of the peptide should move to look more like a real complex. At prediction time we start from a peptide placed at a random position, orientation and shape near the pocket, and let the network move it back toward a believable pose.

One run takes 16 steps (Fig. 2). At each step the network gives four scores: where the whole peptide should shift, how it should rotate, and how its backbone bonds and side-chain bonds should twist. We move the peptide by that amount and add a smaller amount of fresh noise. The noise shrinks on a fixed schedule: translation noise falls from 30 Å to 0.1 Å, rotation noise from 1.65 to 0.03 radians, and bond-angle noise from 3.14 to 0.0314 radians. The last step uses no noise, so the pose settles. We repeat the whole run about 100 times from different random starts, which gives about 100 different poses for the later stages to compare.

Fig. 2 | One run of the sampler, step by step. Five of the sixteen steps are shown, and the faint copies are other runs at the same step. The drawings are an illustration, but the noise values and the schedule underneath are the real ones.

When the user gives a binding site, sampling uses the pocket-known checkpoint. For blind docking it uses the whole-protein checkpoint. Peptides of 13 residues or more can use an optional long-peptide checkpoint, and they fall back to the standard one with a warning if it is not installed. Sampling runs in its own software environment, separate from the scoring tools, and only file paths and settings cross between the two. On a free Colab T4, 100 poses of a 12-residue peptide take about 6 minutes.

Stages 1.5 to 3: relief, ranking and clustering

Diffusion models sometimes place side-chain atoms too close together. Before anything is scored, every pose gets a light clash-relief pass in OpenMM with the AMBER ff14SB force field and implicit solvent. Each heavy atom is held to its starting position by a strong spring (50,000 kJ/mol/nm², which costs about 60 kcal/mol per ångström of movement), so only atoms under severe repulsion move. If any heavy atom moves more than 0.5 Å, we throw the result away and keep the original pose. We chose this because unrestrained minimization let the peptide refold and made the scores worse.

Diffusion sampling covers the whole protein, so some poses land on unrelated surfaces. When the user gives a binding site, we drop poses whose Cα centroid is more than 35 Å from it, and we enlarge the search box, up to 100 Å, if the surviving poses extend past it. Blind mode skips this filter.

Poses are then converted to PDBQT format with meeko and passed to AutoDock Vina. Vina has a narrow job here. When a pose scores positive, meaning it overlaps the protein, Vina's local optimizer resolves the overlap. Its score is logged but is not the reported energy. Each pose is also checked for clashes, meaning any peptide–protein heavy-atom pair closer than 1.5 Å, and ordered by two scorers: BSA-fit, which rewards buried interface surface and penalizes clashes, and a learned ranker that predicts each pose's distance from the native structure. The top of that list becomes best_pose.pdb.

Hundreds of poses are mostly repeats of a few binding modes, so we group them. Each pose is reduced to the Cα atoms near the protein, a pairwise RMSD matrix is built, and average-linkage clustering groups the poses. The number of clusters, from 2 up to 15 but never more than a fifth of the number of poses, is chosen by the silhouette score. The result is a short list of distinct binding modes instead of a hundred near-duplicates. An optional extra step (--refine-topk) relaxes the top cluster representatives with MM-GBSA as an independent physics-based estimate.

Fig. 3 | What each stage hands to the next. Sampling runs in its own software environment (dashed box). The labels on the arrows are the files that pass between stages.

Stage 3.6: the binding energy

The binding energy we report is not a physics calculation. It comes from a learned model that reads the pose (Fig. 4). Each pose is described by 262 numbers: 16 interface-geometry terms (buried hydrophobic area, hydrogen-bond and salt-bridge exposed area, aromatic contacts, mean burial, a Miyazawa–Jernigan contact energy and pocket descriptors) plus about 246 ProtDCal descriptors of the peptide and its interface. A histogram-based gradient-boosted regression tree ensemble turns those numbers into ΔG in kcal/mol.

The model was trained on 633 poses generated by RAPiDock itself, and its internal cross-validation correlation is 0.49. Peptides of eight residues or fewer go to a smaller three-feature model instead, because they make too few interface contacts for the full feature set. Vina and AutoDock4 scores are not part of the reported energy.

Fig. 4 | From a pose to a ΔG. Features are routed by peptide length to one of two models. ΔG feeds selectivity, and variants of one peptide use a separate mutation head.

Blind docking

When the user does not know where the peptide binds, they leave out --site and add --blind. HybriDock-Pep then finds its own candidate sites in three automatic stages. First, an exploratory pass places 300 poses across the whole protein using the whole-protein checkpoint. Second, the Cα centroids of those poses are grouped in space into 3 candidate sites, ranked by how many poses landed there. Third, each site gets its own focused 150-pose run on a cropped neighbourhood of the protein. Only the refined poses go on to scoring, and a file called pocket_assignment.csv records which site each pose came from.

Blind docking costs about three to five times as much as a targeted run. On 120 whole proteins with no site given, our rank-1 pose had a median error of 4.81 Å against 6.50 Å for RAPiDock, and the best of 24 poses was within 5 Å for 102 of 120 proteins against 80 of 120.

Selectivity

For a diagnostic, a binder that sticks to the target is not enough. It must ignore look-alike proteins. The selectivity command docks the same peptide against a target and an off-target, such as the malaria enzyme PfLDH and human LDH, and reports ΔΔG = ΔG(target) − ΔG(off-target) with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval over the top cluster representatives (Fig. 5). A negative ΔΔG whose interval does not cross zero means the peptide is statistically selective.

We lean on ΔΔG because the model's systematic bias affects both proteins the same way and cancels in the difference. Across our tests, selectivity correlates with measurement at about 0.30 to 0.45.

Fig. 5 | From about 100 poses to a selectivity decision. The scores are made up for illustration. The steps, the ΔΔG definition and the bootstrap are how the software works.

Outputs

Every run writes its results to the output folder: ranked_poses.csv (every pose with its scores and cluster), best_pose.pdb (the top-ranked pose), cluster_summary.csv (statistics for each binding mode), convergence_plot.png and silhouette_plot.png (how the clusters were chosen) and run_metadata.json (the settings, versions and seed). Blind runs also write pocket_assignment.csv.

03AI Model Integration

The sampler

The sampling network turns a noisy peptide–protein complex into four scores (Fig. 6). Its input is two graphs and a noise level. The protein graph has one node per residue, with a Cα point and a side-chain tip point, the residue type, its dihedral angles and an ESM-2 language-model embedding. The peptide graph is built the same way, and the two are joined by edges between every peptide and protein pair that is close enough, with the cutoff shrinking as the pose sharpens. The noise level is added to every node and edge so the network knows how much to trust the current pose.

Four stacked layers pass messages inside the peptide, inside the protein, and across the interface. The layers are equivariant, meaning that if you rotate the whole input, the outputs rotate the same way, so the network never has to learn that a rotated complex is still the same complex. Four heads then read the peptide's features: one for moving and rotating the whole peptide, and one each for backbone and side-chain bond twists. The whole model has 7.56 million parameters, small enough to run on a laptop.

Fig. 6 | The sampler's network. Three graphs and a noise embedding enter four equivariant layers, and four heads output the scores that drive each diffusion step.

We changed three things from the original model. We trained on a quality-filtered set: the full corpus of 17,232 complexes gave a median error of 3.03 Å, but removing the 20% with the least buried interface in each length class (13,875 complexes) gave 2.01 Å, which matched hand curation on three times the data. We tested a model six times larger and it trained to no lower loss, so we kept the small one. And we added a blind-docking mode, an optional long-peptide checkpoint, and support for AMD, Intel and Apple GPUs, including a fused Metal kernel that is 3.2 times faster on Apple hardware.

The scorer

The scorer is described under Stage 3.6. What matters here is how it connects to the sampler: it reads the poses the sampler produces, not crystal structures, and it reports its answer in kcal/mol so that it can be compared with experiment.

Why train on AI poses

A model tuned on crystal structures reaches a correlation of 0.585 on crystal poses, but it falls apart on poses generated by RAPiDock, because the features it learned do not occur in generated poses. We call this the AI haircut. Training the scorer on generated poses absorbs that shift. On the same complexes, the model trained on generated poses reaches 0.49 to 0.53, against 0.325 for a method that ignores the pose entirely. The crystal-tuned model is still included, as the crystal-score command, for the case where the input really is a crystal pose.

Short peptides and uncertainty

Peptides of eight residues or fewer make few contacts with the protein, and the full model fails on them (a correlation near zero). A smaller three-feature model recovers a correlation of about 0.51 for them. For all peptides, the typical error is 1.35 to 1.54 kcal/mol, which at room temperature is a factor of about 10 in the dissociation constant. Across different proteins, absolute accuracy tops out at a correlation of about 0.32, which is the ceiling for methods that are not free-energy simulations.

Two things improve on that. If a user has two or three measured affinities for the same protein, anchoring the predictions to them raises the within-protein correlation from about 0.25 to 0.47 up to about 0.7. And for variants of a single peptide, a separate head trained on 4,956 mutations (SKEMPI v2) predicts the change in binding with a correlation of 0.385. We built it because the absolute model barely notices a one-residue change.

04Implementation Details

Install

On macOS, Linux or Windows (through WSL2), installation is three commands: clone the repository, move into the folder, and run ./install.sh (or install.bat on Windows, which sets up WSL2). The script installs everything, including conda, PyTorch and RAPiDock, in about 15 to 30 minutes. The terminal can look frozen during this time, but the work is running in the background, so don't close it. Afterwards, activate the environment with conda activate score-env. To update, run git pull, git submodule update --init --recursive and ./install.sh again. To remove everything, run ./uninstall.sh, or ./uninstall.sh --all to delete the folder too.

HybriDock-Pep needs two software environments because its sampling and scoring tools have version requirements that cannot be solved together. The installer builds both.

For people who don't want to install anything, the same software runs in a Google Colab notebook on a free T4 GPU. The notebook clones the repository, builds both environments in about 17 minutes, and then runs 100 poses in about 10 to 12 minutes. The setup script detects the GPU Colab assigns and installs the matching CUDA build. During setup it also builds RAPiDock's lookup tables and downloads the 2.5 GB ESM-2 language model in the background, so the first docking run does not stall.

A first run

To check an installation, score a known complex. This takes a few seconds and needs no sampling:

hybridock-pep crystal-score \
    --receptor data/pdbs/1YCR_mdm2.pdb \
    --peptide-pdb data/pdbs/1YCR_peptide.pdb \
    --peptide ETFSDLWKLLPE

A good installation gives a ΔG of about −9.3 kcal/mol (the measured value is about −8.5), and anything from −8 to −11 counts as correct. Then run a real docking:

hybridock-pep dock \
    --peptide ETFSDLWKLLPE \
    --receptor data/pdbs/1YCR_mdm2.pdb \
    --site 25.20 -25.61 -7.97 \
    --box 30 \
    --n-samples 20 \
    --output-dir runs/demo

--site is the center of the binding pocket in ångströms and must match the protein you pass in. The results are written to runs/demo/best_pose.pdb and runs/demo/ranked_poses.csv.

Website and terminal UI

HybriDock-Pep has two friendlier ways to run. The website runs on the user's own machine: ./launch_web.sh opens it at http://127.0.0.1:8000 (double-click HybriDock-Pep.command on macOS or launch_web.bat on Windows). It offers three jobs, predicting binding, comparing two proteins, and scoring a structure, around a floating 3D protein. A Guided mode explains each step in plain language and an Expert mode shows the exact coordinates. In the binding-site step the user can drag a box over the protein to choose where the peptide binds, or let the software find the pocket. A small status chip shows whether the run is using a GPU or CPU and how much memory it has left.

The terminal UI (./launch_ui.sh, or ./launch_ui.sh --demo for a short demonstration) is a guided form. AI scoring offers Full (100 poses), Half (50) and Quick (25), plus Selectivity and Demo, and Crystal scoring finishes in seconds. Both also run inside Colab.

Testing and reproducibility

The repository has over 1,000 automated tests and more than 300 numbered experiments, and every number in our results has a command that regenerates it. make verify runs the math-only tests in about 30 seconds without any data. The reproducibility command runs the pipeline with several seeds and reports how well the poses agree, and the seed is recorded in run_metadata.json. Sampling on a GPU is not perfectly repeatable even with a fixed seed: two identical runs differ by about 2.9 Å in mean pose RMSD, which comes from the GPU, not from a bug. The code and data are archived on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.21764713 and 10.5281/zenodo.21680573).

05Results & Validation

How we benchmark

Many published scoring tools report high correlations (0.5 to 0.77) on test sets that overlap with their training data. Once homologous complexes are removed, the field falls to about 0.32. We avoid that trap in three ways. First, we cluster complexes by sequence identity and hold out whole clusters, so no homolog of a test protein is ever in training. Second, we use an identity measure that accounts for where residues sit. An earlier version that ignored placement collapsed 925 peptides into 21 clusters and made accuracy look worse than it was, and we fixed it on 9 July 2026 and kept both results on record. Third, we compare against a faithful rebuild of PPI-Affinity on the identical split, because its web server has been down since 2022. We also report every identity cutoff, not one chosen split. As a check, the clustered correlation (0.35) is lower than the leaky random-split one (0.44), which is what a leakage-free test should show.

For poses, we use a set of 247 peptide complexes filtered against our training data (one per 90%-identity cluster, spread across nine bands of binding-site similarity) as our headline, and a larger length-balanced set of 387 complexes to see how each method behaves from short to long peptides.

Where it loses

We want a reviewer who finds a weakness to find that we already reported it. On PPI-Affinity's own 48-complex test set, the real tool ranks better than we do (r 0.549 against 0.225), though our error is the second lowest. Our lead over RAPiDock is small, 0.12 Å in median pose error. It is large when a test protein's binding site resembles our training data (16 points more complexes within 5 Å above 90% identity) and gone when it does not (equal below 30%). Boltz-2 beats us at picking the top pose in every length class on the larger set, but its training data covers those structures, so we treat it as a ceiling and not a competitor.

Designed binders are hard for every method: on 14 pairs from Dr. Coventry's designed-binder set our median error was 5.93 Å, with none within 2 Å. Our shipped pose ranker is no better than random at choosing the best pose, which is why the top-1 numbers above use a separate consensus ranker. MM-GBSA, the physics-based option, ranks worse than the learned scorer (r about 0.25 against 0.32). We also retired an earlier scorer that generalized to a negative correlation on a small test set, and withdrew a result of r about 0.96 that turned out to use three measured values to estimate a fourth, with the scorer not involved at all.

What the error means

An error of ±1.4 kcal/mol is a factor of about 10.6 in the dissociation constant at 25 °C, so one prediction is really a range. The table shows what the energies mean as dissociation constants.

MDM2 and p53, as a worked example

MDM2 and the p53 peptide (ETFSDLWKLLPE) are a standard test case with a known structure (PDB 1YCR) and a measured binding energy of about −8.5 kcal/mol. Our crystal-score command gives about −9.3. When we dock the peptide from scratch against MDM2 (site 25.20, −25.61, −7.97), the pose lands 0.80 Å from the crystal pose, against 3.54 Å for DiffPepDock. The commands under A first run reproduce this example, and it is the one we recommend for a first test of the website.

The Coventry grid

In mid-September we reached out to Dr. Brian Coventry at the University of Washington, who gave us a challenge: reproduce the 18 × 18 specificity grid from Wu et al. (Science, 2025, Fig. 2B). Given 18 designed binders and 18 peptides, the software must say which peptide belongs to which binder. We struggled for nearly three hours, and solved it by combining our tools with Boltz-2 co-folding.

Our shortlist contained the true partner in 15 of the 18 rows, and called it first in 7 of them (Fig. 9). The whole shortlist was 48 cells out of 324, so the grid tells you to test 48 pairs instead of 324. We missed three rows (pc18, n4 and pc26). The challenge also exposed a number of real bugs in our pipeline, which we fixed. It showed that our software struggles with designed proteins that have deep, conformational grooves, and with proteins that depend on co-folding.

Fig. 9 | The 18 × 18 specificity grid. Left: the measured ground truth from Wu et al. (2025), Fig. 2B. Right: our shortlist, with each row drawn only as deep as you would have to test to reach the true partner. One dark cell means we called the true partner first.

Limits

Absolute binding energy across different proteins is a coarse readout (a correlation of about 0.32), and the relative scores, selectivity and anchored energies, are the strong outputs. Designed binders with deep grooves are our weakest case. HybriDock-Pep predicts how tightly a peptide binds, not how fast. The command line accepts only the 20 standard amino acids, although the underlying sampler supports 92 residue types including D-amino acids and common modifications. Picking the single best pose is weaker than sampling it. And we have not yet validated a finished binder in the wet lab.
